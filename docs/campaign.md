# Campaign, people, and story

Selected design v1, 2026-09-06. Read [DESIGN.md](../DESIGN.md) and [mechanics](mechanics.md) for the core rules.

## Starting a campaign

The default beginning is East Borough on 2028-09-01: 100 affiliated households, a rented shtiebel, an evening study group, and 100 externally arranged school places for 120 children. The court has $180,000 in treasury and an initial policy of preserving 13 weeks of ordinary operating costs. Existing operations have a small positive weekly balance, so the opening is an opportunity to improve a community rather than an immediate insolvency trap.

There are four walkable blocks and 24 visible properties. Eight properties can become relevant to the first chapter; others supply homes, shops, neighbors, and later opportunities. The court has a main hall and side room. An annex and a partner's room are the first accessible alternatives. Exact campaign prices will be tuned from the mechanical fixture and early playtests; its $10,000 capital envelope is an isolated rules experiment, not the whole court's treasury.

The player chooses a court name, emblem, and one of three original musical motifs. The default founding group is balanced. Learning First and Open Table are alternatives in the same menu; they alter the starting capabilities and commitments, not the victory rules. All three are available from the first launch without grinding an unlock. Each starting group includes an adult family member who could become a spiritual successor and an assistant who can be trained for administration; availability and support can still change through play.

The founding rebbe is 48. The player does not control an avatar. The opening cast is fictional:

The exact depiction of religious roles and occasions receives informed cultural review before content production. Fictional characters have substantial individual interests; the cast is not a set of claims about actual people or communities.

## The first 30 minutes

Times below are intended player experience, including pauses, not forced timers.

| Time | What the player does | What the game demonstrates |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0–4 minutes | Inspect the hall, meet the administrator, see the existing study reservation and school-place gap. | The world is readable and already functioning. |
| 4–10 minutes | Draft a school plan and encounter an actual room or schedule conflict. | A worthwhile constraint and an opportunity to discover reuse. |
| 10–16 minutes | Compare sharing the hall, leasing the annex, and negotiating access to another room. | Different viable plans consume different resources and preserve different opportunities. |
| 16–21 minutes | Commit, run time, and observe the program begin. | Forecasts and outcomes agree; people visibly benefit. |
| 21–27 minutes | Respond to one circumstance that depends on the chosen plan: a workshop offer, a partner's request, or an available gathering date. | The first solution changes the next decision. |
| 27–30 minutes | Host or witness a modest payoff, then choose the next project toward a permanent home. | Accomplishment, a remembered person, and a reason to continue. |

Instruction appears only for a newly encountered operation and can be dismissed. The tutorial accepts any valid plan. It does not force the player to follow a fixed sequence of exact placements. A repeatable standalone challenge exposes the small rules fixture separately.

## Chapter conditions

The chapters organize pacing and introduce capabilities; their boundaries are not walls around the simulation.

### 1. A place of our own

Complete the first capacity problem, run a program reliably for 13 weeks, establish one useful agreement, and acquire a permanent home. Acquisition may be a purchase, a financed purchase, or a long-term institutional ownership partnership. The chapter ends with a visible opening whose contributors come from actual play.

New tools: planning, room reuse, capacity, staffing, commitments, forecasts, and basic property improvement.

### 2. A neighborhood that works

Operate one teaching, one welcome, and one provide program; maintain at least 80% housing and school coverage among participating households for two quarters; and complete two distinct local partnerships. The player chooses the particular programs and how much to own directly.

New tools: functional combinations between properties, apprentices, local business arrangements, civic projects, and small celebrations with differing requirements.

### 3. Beyond the block

Establish the first satellite campus, appoint a steward, and complete a reliable inter-branch visit or service route. The first satellite can be a small hosted community within a partner's building. It need not duplicate the home campus.

New tools: delegated budgets, branch priorities, limited transport, shared regional facilities, and different local demand. Four regional district conditions are available: dense East Borough, commercially strong Market Park, expanding Outer Ward, and spacious but dispersed Cedar Valley.

### 4. The next generation

Establish a succession charter, train at least one eligible spiritual successor and one administrative successor, and resolve a leadership transition when it arises. The campaign can already be expanding internationally; succession occurs when the characters' lives bring it about.

New tools: long commitments, candidate support, coalition agreements, institutional charters, and autonomous branches. A peaceful handover, a negotiated division, or reconciliation can all preserve the campaign.

### 5. A worldwide court

Open or partner with charter communities in multiple world regions. A city becomes charter-eligible after two quarters with active teaching, welcome, and provide programs; at least 80% local housing and school coverage; a declared operating funding source; and a willing local steward.

New tools: regional hosting and transport packages, reciprocal assistance, adaptation of traditions, and coalition relationships. The detailed planning model persists, but the player delegates its routine execution.

### 6. The Great Gathering

Meet all of the following prerequisites:

- 12 charter cities spanning North America, South America, Europe, Africa, Asia, and Oceania.
- Three independent rival courts willingly participating in the compact.
- Eight consecutive quarters of nonnegative consolidated operating balance, with capital investment accounted for separately.
- A funded 13-week operating reserve and no overdue critical agreement.
- At least one successfully resolved leadership transition.

Start a 26-week preparation project. Assemble six regional packages covering travel, hosts, venues, food, local leadership, and scheduling. The finale is a distributed week of events, with a central gathering at the original court or a site selected by the player.

Success requires every regional package to be valid, at least 90% of agreed guest/service delivery to occur, all hard venue and staff capacities to be respected, and no home branch to lose critical school or housing coverage for two consecutive weeks because of diverted resources.

Plans offer alternate suppliers, staggered arrivals, smaller gatherings, reciprocal hosting, and backup venues. Forecasts reveal likely shortfalls. The finale tests the existing mechanics at scale; it introduces no unrelated action minigame or final persuasion roll.

If delivery falls short, the gathering still takes place at its achieved scale and creates its own history. The player can prepare a new attempt after 13 weeks. Unused recoverable commitments are released according to their actual contracts. A failed attempt does not erase decades of progress.

Victory declares world domination with the Grand Committee for Absolutely Everything. The original building, founding contributions, family histories, and motif anchor the celebration. Optional post-victory projects include a tiny Antarctic outpost and an extravagantly impractical global transport scheme.

## World scope

The world view has 24 destination nodes, four in each of the six regions. The destinations are New York, Los Angeles, Miami, Montreal; Buenos Aires, Sao Paulo, Santiago, Bogota; London, Antwerp, Paris, Vienna; Jerusalem, Singapore, Mumbai, Tokyo; Johannesburg, Cape Town, Nairobi, Casablanca; Melbourne, Sydney, Auckland, and Wellington.

These are settings for fictional institutions and stories, not representations of named real courts. Each node combines authored constraints with seeded opportunities. The player needs 12 of the 24, enabling different routes and partnerships.

All destinations are visible on the world map. Introductions and visits reveal particular local people, terms, and opportunities; no required continent is hidden behind a lucky event draw. Campaign generation guarantees at least one feasible outreach path in each region. A lost contact can be replaced through another introduction or a funded exploratory visit.

Four reusable district layouts and regional facade/prop palettes provide detailed visitable neighborhoods. We are not making 24 literal city reconstructions. A region's institutions serve local people and participating communities without assuming religious or political control of the city's population.

## Story generation and remembered history

The director selects from 120 authored seeds: 40 neighborhood, 28 regional, 28 dynastic, and 24 worldwide. The tone allocation is 64 positive/neutral opportunities or discoveries, 40 social/institutional complications, and 16 material setbacks. Ordinary ambient life is additional and does not consume event slots.

Each seed defines prerequisites, eligible participants, place, opportunity, any decision, visible outcome, and persistent effects. Selection considers current needs, calendar, recent events, relationships, and variety. It does not invent a matching past event merely to make a callback sound personal.

At most three major decision prompts are introduced per quarter, and at most one director-created material setback in two quarters on Standard. Direct consequences of the player's own commitments still apply. Smaller discoveries appear in the world or digest; they do not all demand a response.

An exact seed has a four-quarter minimum cooldown unless it is a recurring calendar occasion. Recurrences require a meaningful difference in participants, place, scale, or consequence to receive a major presentation. Major scenes are capped so the player has room to enjoy them.

Memories keep event IDs, names, dates, places, actual contributions, selected objects, and traditions. The player can name institutions, keep an old sign, display an opening photograph, preserve an annual gathering, and revisit the chronicle. NPC friendships and activities can begin independently within their resources and availability.

## Replay, difficulty, and endings

Campaign seeds vary eligible people, opportunities, property terms, rival priorities, and event order within authored constraints. The street layout is selected from four readable templates. Starting capabilities, branch specializations, partners, succession outcomes, and chosen regions provide further variation.

Gentle starts with 50% more discretionary reserves and an extra quarter of warning for scripted pressure. Standard uses the ordinary tables. Demanding reduces discretionary reserves by 25% and makes counterparties less tolerant of broken commitments. Hard occupancy and accounting rules are identical. The player can change difficulty without losing the save.

Creative sandbox allows funds, aging, event pressure, and speed to be adjusted. It is a distinct expression-focused mode, not evidence that resource management is fun.

Victory, voluntary retirement, or institutional collapse produces a chronicle based on actual history. Death and a communal split are possible transitions within play. Post-victory continuation remains available through the supported calendar, followed by a final-year sandbox.
