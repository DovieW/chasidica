# Designing for fun

Initial planning proposals: 2026-09-06. The creative priority is agreed; the mechanisms below still need exploration and playtesting.

Current priority: use the [game-design principles and tests](game-design-principles.md) to establish repeatable mechanical fun. The experiences described here remain targets, not evidence that the underlying game works.

## The creative priority

The game should offer lots of cool moments and novel experiences: unexpected, joyful, and memorable.

Success means that someone finishing a session can recall something they enjoyed seeing, discovering, causing, or realizing. Numerical progress supports that experience, but does not establish it by itself.

## Sources of enjoyment to combine

| Source | What the player experiences | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Visible accomplishment | Something they worked toward becomes real. | The first permanent building opens and fills with familiar people. |
| Discovery | A place, person, or possibility turns out to contain more than expected. | An unused passage could connect two courtyards. |
| Spontaneous life | People initiate worthwhile things themselves. | A shopkeeper supplies refreshments for a gathering without being assigned a task. |
| Creative agency | An unusual player idea works and changes future possibilities. | Combining a courtyard and kitchen creates a welcoming venue for visiting families. |
| Personal continuity | An earlier relationship or decision gains meaning later. | A former pupil returns as a teacher. |
| Affectionate comedy | Recognizable characters collide with an increasingly absurd situation. | An airline is still governed by the original minibus committee. |
| Novel ways to play | Growth introduces new kinds of decisions and interactions. | A first satellite community brings hosts, travel, and local leadership into play. |
| Anticipation and relief | There is something to look forward to and time to enjoy it. | A long-planned celebration arrives during a stable, prosperous season. |

## How moments might arise

Use a combination of authored occasions, recurring rhythms, discoveries, and interactions between simulated people and places.

- Authored occasions give milestones a satisfying presentation: an opening, a first visit, a wedding, a reunion.
- Recurring rhythms make the neighborhood feel familiar: seasons, the calendar, school days, preparations, and annual traditions.
- Discoveries reveal new options: a capable resident, an overlooked space, a useful contact, a neglected institution.
- Simulated interactions change an occasion's meaning: who attends, who helps, what they remember, and what happens afterward.

A celebration at the same building can feel different when its organizer is a former pupil, a rival unexpectedly attends, or three generations of an early family are present. Those connections need to come from actual campaign history.

Prefer variation in behavior and possibilities as well as words. A new name attached to an identical event is limited novelty.

## Moments should happen in the world

The default presentation should be something the player can see or hear: people arrive, a courtyard fills, a familiar tune is played, a room changes, or a procession moves down a street. A concise notification can help the player find it.

Only ask for a decision when there is a worthwhile choice. Some moments should simply be enjoyable to witness. Let players follow an event at their preferred pace, with a recent-events record for things they missed.

Place, sound, people, and small persistent details can carry a story. Naming a room, keeping an old sign, or continuing a tradition can preserve a player's attachment across generations.

## Make surprise feel earned

Give surprising developments plausible roots in people, place, timing, relationships, and past decisions. The exact outcome can surprise the player while its causes make sense afterward.

Offer a mix of things the player initiates, things they can encourage, and things residents do independently. Leave room for generous and delightful outcomes without an obligatory hidden penalty.

Complications should create interesting choices and stories. If every success immediately produces another emergency, the promised joy becomes difficult to experience.

Novelty should also open new actions. A visiting group might make reciprocal visits possible; a community kitchen might support new kinds of gatherings; a trusted administrator might make a distant branch manageable.

## Let the game remember

A candidate approach is to retain a small set of meaningful facts about people, places, institutions, and traditions:

- Who helped establish a place, and who first used it.
- Which people learned, worked, celebrated, or met there.
- What the player named or chose to preserve.
- Which favors, visits, collaborations, and rivalries actually occurred.
- Which traditions began locally and spread elsewhere.

Later moments can refer back to those facts. The goal is recognizable continuity rather than fabricated personal history or an exhaustive log of every simulated action.

## Pace the experience

Mix ambient pleasures, small surprises, player projects, and larger milestones. Give demanding decisions room to breathe. Larger scale should bring richer relationships and new possibilities, with routine responsibilities delegated.

Avoid rigid event quotas. Novelty fatigue and repetition both matter. A candidate system would consider recent events, ongoing projects, missed opportunities, and whether the player has had time to enjoy a payoff. Exact scheduling rules are undecided.

Recurring celebrations can remain valuable even after their first appearance when the attendees, venue, traditions, or family history have changed.

## A candidate scene to support playtesting

This scene can help test presentation and emotional payoff. A mechanical test also needs explicit actions, constraints, consequences, and alternative approaches, as described in the principles document.

Before a large economic or dynasty simulation, test one small neighborhood with a handful of recurring characters, one adaptable property, and one anticipated communal occasion.

Try to support this short arc:

1. Discover an opportunity in an ordinary place.
2. Make a choice that expresses how you want the community to develop.
3. See someone respond independently.
4. Enjoy a visible payoff.
5. Recognize an earlier person, object, or decision in a later moment.

Include at least one opportunity with more than one viable approach. A scripted presentation can test the intended feeling; repeat play with different choices is needed to test whether the systems can keep producing it.

Useful playtest questions:

- What do you remember from the session?
- What surprised or delighted you?
- Did you feel that your decisions helped bring it about?
- What did you want to try next?
- Which moments felt repeated, intrusive, or arbitrary?
- Did you enjoy watching the neighborhood when no action was required?

The test has not been implemented. These questions are proposed design checks, not evidence that the game is already fun.
